The Jafurah decade: how Saudi gas quietly became an equity story

Aramco spent seven years and $110bn turning a shale basin nobody modelled into 11% of group revenue. The market is still paying for a crude proxy — and the next four quarters are where that gap either closes or doesn't.

THE ARGUMENT IN THREE LINES
Gas is now 11.4% of group revenue, up 730 bp in seven years — and unlike crude it is sold on domestic contracts that reprice annually, not on Brent.
Phase 3 was sanctioned two quarters early at a unit cost 18% below Phase 1 — the learning curve is real and it is compounding.
At 27.15 the shares discount a $62 Brent deck and no gas re-rating. We think the second half of that is wrong.

In the spring of 2019, a Riyadh analyst asked Amin Nasser what Jafurah was worth. The answer, delivered on a conference call with the patience of a man who had heard the question too often, was that it was worth what the gas was worth — and that the gas was for the Kingdom, not for the model. Seven years later the model has caught up. Jafurah is the largest unconventional non-associated gas field in the Middle East, and as of the second quarter it is doing something no Aramco asset has done before: it is changing the shape of the earnings line.

The number that matters is 11.4%. That is the share of group revenue that came from gas and gas liquids in Q1 2026, against 4.1% in 2019. It sounds modest until you consider what it is made of: long-duration domestic supply contracts, indexed to a regulated tariff that has been revised upward twice since 2021, sold into a Kingdom that is electrifying industry and displacing crude burn at the same time. It is, in other words, a utility hiding inside an oil major.

Which brings us to the disagreement. Sell-side models still treat Aramco's gas segment as a cost centre with a revenue line attached — a strategic obligation the Kingdom asked for and the company delivers at low single-digit returns. Our read of the Phase 3 sanction economics says that stopped being true somewhere around the end of Phase 1.

The learning curve is where the argument gets specific. Phase 1 was drilled with imported rigs, imported crews and a completion design borrowed wholesale from the Permian. Phase 2 localised 61% of the supply chain. Phase 3, on the sanction case management put to the board in June, comes in at a unit development cost 18% below Phase 1 in real terms — and the incremental barrel of NGL that comes with it now carries a netback that competes with light crude.

The dividend question is where the desk and the street diverge most sharply. Our FY26 free-cash-flow build covers the base distribution to a $58 Brent deck on unchanged costs — six dollars of headroom, thinner than the company's own language implies but wider than the bears assume. The performance layer is a different matter, and the Q2 suspension told you which of the two management treats as discretionary.

So the position resolves to a single question, and it is not about oil. It is whether a company that has spent seven years learning to drill shale gas in the desert gets paid for having learned it. On 4 August, when the segment split is disclosed for the first time, we find out whether anyone else is looking.
